You: Cinematography Mentor: Avoiding flicker when filming under LED lights involves understanding the interaction between your camera's settings and the electrical characteristics of the LEDs. Here are some key tips:

1. **Match Shutter Speed to the LED Flicker Frequency:**  
   LED lights powered by AC mains often flicker at twice the mains frequency (e.g., 100 Hz in 50 Hz regions, 120 Hz in 60 Hz regions). Using a shutter speed that is not synchronized with this frequency can cause flicker.  
   - For 50 Hz regions, use shutter speeds like 1/50, 1/100, or 1/150 sec.  
   - For 60 Hz regions, use shutter speeds like 1/60, 1/120, or 1/180 sec.

2. **Use Flicker Reduction or Anti-Flicker Settings:**  
   Many modern cameras have built-in flicker reduction or anti-flicker modes that automatically adjust shutter timing to reduce flicker. Enable this feature if available.

3. **Use Continuous (Non-Pulsed) LED Lighting:**  
   Some LED lights designed for video use high-frequency drivers or DC power to avoid flicker. If possible, use professional LED panels designed for cinematography rather than cheap consumer LEDs.

4. **Adjust Frame Rate:**  
   Sometimes changing your frame rate to match the mains frequency cycle helps. For example, shooting at 25 fps in 50 Hz regions or 30 fps in 60 Hz regions.

5. **Use Manual Exposure:**  
   Auto exposure can cause changes in shutter speed or gain that exacerbate flicker. Lock your exposure manually to keep settings consistent.

6. **Test Before Shooting:**  
   Always test your lighting setup and camera settings before the actual shoot to detect flicker early.

If flicker persists despite these steps, consider supplementing or replacing LED sources with tungsten or other continuous lighting types less prone to flicker.

You: Cinematography Mentor: For a moody close-up, lighting is all about creating contrast, depth, and selective illumination to evoke atmosphere and emotion. Here are some effective techniques:

1. **Use Low-Key Lighting:**  
   Keep most of the frame in shadow, lighting only key parts of the face. This creates strong contrast and a dramatic feel.

2. **Single Key Light with Hard Shadows:**  
   Use a single, relatively small, focused light source (like a bare bulb, a small fresnel, or a snooted LED) placed at an angle (e.g., 45 degrees to the side and slightly above the subject) to sculpt the face with shadows. Hard shadows add intensity and texture.

3. **Use Negative Fill:**  
   Place a black flag or dark material on the side opposite the key light to deepen shadows and prevent unwanted fill light.

4. **Control Background and Rim Lighting:**  
   Keep the background dark or subtly lit to avoid distraction. A subtle rim or hair light can help separate the subject from the background without brightening the overall scene.

5. **Use Color Gels or Practicals:**  
   Adding a cool blue or warm amber gel can enhance mood. Alternatively, use practical lights (like a dim lamp or candle) within the frame to add atmosphere.

6. **Adjust Light Position and Distance:**  
   Moving the light closer increases contrast and falloff, emphasizing texture and mood.

7. **Camera Settings:**  
   Use a wide aperture (low f-stop) to create a shallow depth of field, isolating the subject and softening the background.

Example setup: A single key light at 45° angle, slightly above eye level, with a flag on the opposite side for negative fill, and a dark or dimly lit background. Optionally, add a subtle rim light behind the subject for separation.

3. **To Show Multiple Characters in the Frame:**  
   Wide lenses allow you to keep two or more characters in the same shot, especially in confined spaces, without cutting to tighter shots. This can support continuous dialogue and maintain spatial continuity.

4. **To Use Dynamic Camera Movement:**  
   Wide lenses are more forgiving with camera movement and can create dynamic, immersive shots that pull the audience into the scene.

### Considerations and Cautions

- **Distortion:** Wide lenses can distort faces, especially near the edges of the frame. Use this intentionally for stylistic effect but avoid it if you want a natural look.
- **Depth of Field:** Wide lenses generally have a deeper depth of field, so background elements may be more in focus, which can distract if not controlled.
- **Framing:** Because wide lenses capture so much, be mindful of the frame edges to avoid unwanted objects or clutter.

- For full-frame cameras, wide-angle lenses are generally considered 24mm and wider (e.g., 16mm, 18mm, 21mm).
- For APS-C or crop sensors, adjust accordingly (e.g., 16mm on APS-C roughly equals 24mm full-frame equivalent).
